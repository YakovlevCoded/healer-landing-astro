Vitamin B12 deficiency is easy to miss because its symptoms — tiredness, fog, low mood — are so common and so easily blamed on everything else. But B12 does something no other nutrient quite does: it's essential for both healthy nerves and healthy red blood cells, which is why a deficiency can quietly cause neurological symptoms *and* anemia at the same time.

## The stat most people don't know

Vitamin B12 insufficiency affects roughly **1 in 8 adults over 60** — about 12% by one measure — according to [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminB12-HealthProfessional/) data, and it's more common still in vegetarians, vegans, and people on long-term metformin or acid-reducing medication. Yet B12 is rarely tested unless someone specifically asks — so it's a common, correctable cause of fatigue and fog that slips through.

## The signs

- **Fatigue and weakness** — the most common and least specific
- **Brain fog** and difficulty concentrating ([more on brain-fog markers](/blog/brain-fog-blood-markers/))
- **Tingling or numbness** in hands and feet — a more specific neurological sign
- **Balance problems**
- **Sore or inflamed tongue**
- **Mood changes** or low mood
- In blood work: a particular kind of anemia (large red blood cells)

The neurological symptoms matter because, left long enough, some can become lasting — which is why catching it early is worth the test.

## Who's at risk

- **Older adults** — stomach acid and absorption decline with age
- **Vegetarians and vegans** — B12 comes almost entirely from animal foods
- **People on metformin or long-term acid reducers** (PPIs, H2 blockers) — both impair absorption
- **Digestive conditions** — pernicious anemia, celiac, Crohn's, or after gut surgery

If you're in one of these groups, it's worth checking B12 even without clear symptoms.

## How it's tested

- **Serum B12** — the first-line test. Useful, but the "normal" range is wide and borderline results are common.
- **MMA (methylmalonic acid) and homocysteine** — these rise when B12 is *functionally* low, so they help confirm a true deficiency when serum B12 sits in the gray zone.

As with [iron](/blog/full-iron-panel/), the context around the single number is what makes it reliable.

## What to do

1. **Ask for serum B12** — and MMA/homocysteine if it's borderline or symptoms persist.
2. **Know your risk group** — older age, plant-based diet, metformin/acid reducers all warrant checking.
3. **Don't ignore neurological signs** — tingling and numbness deserve prompt attention.
4. **Talk to a clinician** about the cause and whether oral or injected B12 is appropriate.
