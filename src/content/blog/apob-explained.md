Most people's cholesterol conversation stops at LDL. But there's a marker that captures cardiovascular risk more precisely — one that major guidelines increasingly favor, yet that rarely appears on a standard panel unless you ask. It's **ApoB**, and understanding it changes how you read your lipids.

## What ApoB actually measures

Here's the key idea: what clogs arteries isn't cholesterol floating freely — it's cholesterol packaged in **particles** that burrow into artery walls. Every one of those artery-clogging (atherogenic) particles — LDL, VLDL, and others — carries exactly **one ApoB protein**.

So measuring ApoB **counts the total number of risk-carrying particles** in your blood. And it turns out the *number* of particles drives plaque and heart-attack risk more than the total cholesterol they contain.

## The stat most people don't know

The **2019 ESC/EAS guidelines concluded that ApoB is a more accurate marker of cardiovascular risk than either LDL-C or non-HDL cholesterol**, and a 2025 review found 9 of 9 studies rated ApoB as more accurate than LDL cholesterol ([Journal of the American Heart Association](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/JAHA.122.025858)). When ApoB and LDL-C disagree, ApoB is the better predictor of risk.

## Why LDL cholesterol can mislead

Two people can have the **same LDL cholesterol** but a very different *number* of LDL particles. The one with more, smaller particles carries more ApoB — and more risk — despite an identical LDL-C number. This mismatch is most common exactly where it matters most: in people with **high [triglycerides](/blog/triglycerides-explained/), diabetes, or obesity** — the [insulin-resistant](/blog/how-to-know-if-you-have-insulin-resistance/) profile. In those cases, LDL cholesterol can quietly underestimate true risk, and ApoB catches it.

## What the numbers mean

- **Below ~90 mg/dL** — general target for average risk
- **Below ~80, or even ~65 mg/dL** — targets used for higher-risk individuals
- Thresholds depend on your **overall risk profile**, so interpret with a clinician

The catch: ApoB isn't on most standard lipid panels. You typically have to **request it by name.**

## What to do

1. **Ask for ApoB by name** — it's usually not included by default.
2. **Prioritize it if you have high triglycerides, diabetes, or extra weight** — that's where LDL-C most underestimates risk.
3. **Read it with your full panel**, not in isolation.
4. **Talk to a clinician** to set a target appropriate to your overall risk.
