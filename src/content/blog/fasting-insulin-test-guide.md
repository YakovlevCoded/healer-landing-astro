There's a blood test that can flag metabolic trouble up to a decade before a standard checkup would — and most people have never had it run. It's not exotic or expensive. It's fasting insulin, and it's one of the most useful early-warning markers in all of routine blood work. It just isn't part of the standard screening most doctors order.

## Why insulin, not just glucose

Your body's first response to rising blood sugar isn't to let glucose climb — it's to release **more insulin** to push that sugar into cells. So in the early stages of insulin resistance, glucose stays perfectly normal *because insulin is quietly working overtime to keep it there.*

That's the catch with standard screening. A fasting glucose test and even [HbA1c](/blog/hba1c-explained/) measure the sugar — the thing that stays normal longest. Fasting insulin measures the effort your body is spending to keep it normal. Which means insulin starts rising **years before** glucose does.

## The stat most people don't know

The CDC estimates about **96 million US adults — more than 1 in 3 — have prediabetes, and over 80% don't know it** ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/php/data-research/index.html)). A big reason for the blind spot: the standard tests measure the lagging signal. By the time fasting glucose or HbA1c crosses into the abnormal range, insulin resistance has often been building for **10 to 15 years** ([StatPearls / NCBI](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK507839/)) — a window in which a fasting insulin test could have caught it while it was still easy to reverse.

## What the numbers mean

- **Standard "normal":** labs often go up to ~25 µIU/mL — a very wide range.
- **Optimal (metabolic-health view):** many clinicians target **under ~6–8 µIU/mL** fasting.
- **Worth attention:** fasting insulin in the teens *with normal glucose* — a classic sign your body is compensating.

Combined with fasting glucose, fasting insulin is what lets you calculate **HOMA-IR**, the practical insulin-resistance score. That's why the two together tell you far more than glucose alone. ([Full guide: how to know if you have insulin resistance](/blog/how-to-know-if-you-have-insulin-resistance/).)

If you've noticed that every source quotes a different cutoff, there's a concrete reason for it — insulin assays were never standardized between laboratories. [Why fasting insulin ranges disagree](/blog/fasting-insulin-range/) covers what that means for reading your own result.

## What to do

1. **Ask specifically for fasting insulin** alongside fasting glucose — it's usually not included by default, so you have to request it by name.
2. **Fast properly** (typically 8–12 hours) — insulin is sensitive to recent food, so the draw needs to be genuinely fasting.
3. **Get HOMA-IR calculated** from the pair, and look at the trend, not one reading.
4. **Talk to a clinician** to interpret it in context — especially with family history of diabetes or extra weight around the middle.
