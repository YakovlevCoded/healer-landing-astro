Losing more hair than usual is alarming, and the default assumption is often genetics or age. But a large share of sudden or diffuse shedding — the kind where hair thins all over rather than in a pattern — has a **testable cause sitting in routine blood work.** Before resigning yourself to it, it's worth checking the markers that most commonly drive it.

## The stat most people don't know

In studies of women with **telogen effluvium** — the medical term for diffuse hair shedding — average ferritin levels were markedly lower than in women without shedding (one study found means of about 24 ng/mL in cases versus 45 ng/mL in controls), and low serum ferritin is significantly associated with the condition ([Assessment of Serum Ferritin in Telogen Effluvium, NCBI](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12839778/)). The catch: much of that low ferritin sits inside the lab's "normal" range, so it's rarely flagged as the cause.

## The markers worth checking

- **Ferritin (iron stores)** — the big one for hair. While anemia is defined lower, the hair-regrowth target many dermatologists use is **≥40–50 ng/mL**, well above the lab's floor. ([What low ferritin means](/blog/low-ferritin-guide/); [AAFP](https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2013/0115/p98.html).)
- **Thyroid (TSH ± free T4)** — both under- and overactive thyroid cause diffuse thinning, usually reversible once corrected. ([High TSH, explained](/blog/high-tsh-thyroid-explained/); [NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/endocrine-diseases/hypothyroidism).)
- **Vitamin D** — deficiency is linked to hair-cycle disruption and several forms of hair loss ([NIH ODS](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminD-HealthProfessional/)).
- **Vitamin B12 and zinc** — deficiencies can contribute, especially in vegetarians, vegans, and after rapid weight loss.

Often it's not one marker but a combination — low-ish ferritin plus a borderline thyroid, for example — that tips hair into shedding.

## Why timing confuses people

Telogen effluvium has a frustrating quirk: the shedding usually shows up **2–4 months after** the trigger (an illness, crash diet, stress, childbirth, or a nutrient dip). So by the time your hair is visibly thinning, the blood-work cause may have been building for months — which is exactly why a single "how are things now" test can miss it, and why tracking your markers over time matters.

## What to do

1. **Ask for ferritin, thyroid (TSH), vitamin D, B12, and zinc** — name ferritin specifically, since the "normal" floor hides hair-relevant lows.
2. **Get your real numbers** and compare against functional targets (ferritin ≥40–50 for hair), not just the lab range.
3. **Be patient and track** — shedding lags its cause by months, and regrowth after correcting a deficiency also takes months. Re-test at ~3 months.
4. **See a clinician or dermatologist** — especially if shedding is heavy, patterned, or persistent.
