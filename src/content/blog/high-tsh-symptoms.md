A high TSH means your pituitary is working overtime to prod an [underactive thyroid](/blog/high-tsh-thyroid-explained/). Since thyroid hormone sets your body's metabolic pace, an underactive thyroid tends to slow everything down — energy, temperature, digestion, mood. But the symptoms are famously vague, which is exactly why they're so often blamed on stress, aging, or "just being busy."

## The classic symptoms

An elevated TSH (underactive thyroid) commonly produces ([NIDDK](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/endocrine-diseases/hypothyroidism); [American Thyroid Association](https://www.thyroid.org/hypothyroidism/)):

- **Fatigue** that doesn't improve with sleep
- **Unexplained weight gain** or difficulty losing weight ([the blood markers behind weight gain](/blog/weight-gain-blood-markers/))
- **Cold intolerance** — feeling cold when others are comfortable ([cold hands and feet](/blog/cold-hands-feet-labs/))
- **Dry skin and thinning hair** ([hair loss and blood work](/blog/hair-loss-blood-markers/))
- **Constipation**
- **Brain fog** and difficulty concentrating ([brain fog markers](/blog/brain-fog-blood-markers/))
- **Low mood** or depression
- **Heavier or irregular periods**
- **Muscle aches and slowed heart rate**

Because these overlap with so many other things (including low iron and low vitamin D), the thyroid connection is easy to miss until someone looks at the TSH.

## The stat most people don't know

Here's the twist: **you can have a high TSH and feel completely fine.** In subclinical hypothyroidism — a mildly elevated TSH (roughly 4–10) with still-normal thyroid hormone — many people have few or no symptoms, and undiagnosed hypothyroidism (mostly this milder form) affects several percent of adults ([Ettleson et al., J Clin Endocrinol Metab](https://academic.oup.com/jcem/article/109/2/e657/7279439)). That's why treatment decisions weigh symptoms *and* the number *and* the trend — not the TSH alone.

## Why symptoms and the number don't always match

There's no clean threshold where symptoms switch on. It depends on how sensitive you are, how quickly TSH rose, and your free T4 level. Someone can feel rough at a TSH of 6; another feels fine at 9. This mismatch is the whole reason a high TSH is best interpreted **in context** — beside free T4, antibodies, and how you actually feel, tracked over time. (For the reasons TSH rises in the first place, see [what causes high TSH](/blog/high-tsh-causes/).)

## What to do

1. **Note your symptoms** — and get your actual TSH and free T4 numbers, not just "normal."
2. **Check for the cause** — ask about TPO antibodies (Hashimoto's is the usual reason).
3. **Track symptoms against the trend** — a rising TSH with worsening symptoms is more actionable than one reading.
4. **Talk to a clinician** — especially if TSH is above 10 or symptoms are significant.
